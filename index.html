03 · Churn Prediction — Telecom Subscription

Elevated churn with no structural understanding of where attrition is concentrated or what drives it. Objective: move from aggregate churn reporting to actionable segmentation with testable interventions. Six-branch MECE issue tree. GBM classifier ROC-AUC 0.842.

PythonScikit-LearnGradient BoostingCohort AnalysisPublic dataset
989 customers isolated65.8% churn · £85/month avg · 4 A/B test designs
Six-branch issue tree
Branch 1 — Tenure: 0–6 month customers churn at 52.9%. Established (24+ months): 14.0%. Front-loaded — concentrated in the onboarding and early-life period. Changes the intervention strategy entirely: broad loyalty programmes are the wrong tool.

Branch 2 — Contract structure: Month-to-month 42.7% · Two-year 2.8%. A 15× structural difference attributable entirely to commitment mechanism.

Branch 3 — Service depth: Zero add-on services: 43.8% churn. All four services: 5.3%. Each additional service creates a switching cost and reduces propensity to leave.

Branch 4 — Differentiating services: Online Security and Tech Support show a +17pp adoption gap between churners and non-churners (statistically significant). These create a support relationship that increases perceived switching cost.

Branch 5 — Risk segment: Progressive filter (month-to-month + tenure <12 months + zero security services) → 989 customers, 65.8% churn, £85/month average charges. High-risk and high-value.

Branch 6 — Predictive model: Gradient Boosting, ROC-AUC 0.842 holdout / 0.842 ± 0.008 cross-validated. Feature importance: contract type 35%, tenure 18%, monthly charges 12%.
Four A/B test designs
1. Contract upgrade offer at Day 30 and Day 90 — targets the commitment mechanism directly
2. Free 3-month Online Security trial at onboarding — addresses the highest-impact service adoption gap
3. Autopay migration incentive (5% discount) — reduces passive churn from payment failure
4. Proactive retention outreach to 989-customer risk segment at Month 1 and Month 3

Each designed with defined control group, success metric, sample size, and measurement timeframe.
Limitations: single cross-sectional snapshot — no longitudinal view of individual risk state transitions · no stated churn reasons — behavioural signals only · all findings observational; causal claims require the A/B tests to execute.

04 · SaaS Churn Diagnostic — Productivity App

Founder proposed reducing prices to address elevated churn. Engagement objective: validate or refute the pricing hypothesis using behavioural data before committing to a revenue-reducing intervention. The conclusion was the opposite of what he expected.

PythonPandasMixpanel exportCohort AnalysisReal client data
−18% churn30-day reduction · A/B tested · 8-week window · clean control
The finding
Segmented churners by tenure cohort (0–30, 31–90, 90+ days) crossed with feature usage intensity. Primary finding: customers churning within 0–30 days showed near-zero engagement with the core product feature. They never experienced the value proposition — they failed to activate, not to perceive value relative to price.
Validation of the pricing hypothesis
Highest-tier customers (paying the most) exhibited the lowest churn rates. If pricing were the causal mechanism, this relationship would be inverted — the most price-exposed segment would show the highest attrition. The data refuted the pricing hypothesis directly. Root cause: activation failure in the onboarding sequence. Intervention: product experience improvement, not price reduction.
Stakeholder management
Rather than directly challenging the founder's hypothesis, the approach was to present the tier-level retention data and allow the conclusion to emerge from the evidence. The founder independently recognised the implication — that fixing onboarding represented a lower-cost, lower-risk intervention than repricing — which generated immediate buy-in for implementation without a contested recommendation.
Result
Designed a Day-3 triggered email for users who had not activated the core feature by that point. Founder implemented as a controlled A/B test: 50/50 random split, 8-week measurement window. Result: 18% reduction in 30-day churn in the treatment group.
Limitations: directional attribution only — A/B design was clean with no concurrent external changes but sample size was modest and timeframe short; documented explicitly in handoff · single intervention tested; the relative contribution of email timing versus content versus subject line is not isolated.

05 · Inventory Optimisation — 3,788-SKU Distributor

Working capital tied up in slow-moving stock while fast-moving SKUs experienced recurrent stockouts. No classification framework existed. ABC-XYZ analysis: the algorithm flagged 811 candidates for rationalisation. Commercial judgment reduced that to 19.

PythonSQLExcelABC-XYZReorder Point ModelPublic dataset
19 vs 811 candidatesAlgorithm · judgment · 22 high-revenue/high-variance SKUs flagged
Classification results
ABC: 816 SKUs (22% of catalogue) generate 80% of £10.2M revenue.
XYZ: 46% of SKUs are Z-class (CV > 1.0). Demand variance exceeds the mean — standard replenishment logic is unreliable for these items. Typical for seasonal giftware wholesale: customers order in bursts around events, not steadily.
Reorder model: ROP = (μ × LT) + (z × σ × √LT). Service level targets: A-class 98% (z=2.054), B-class 95% (z=1.645), C-class 90% (z=1.281).
Key finding: 22 of the top 80 revenue-generating SKUs exhibit CV > 1.2 — high revenue contribution combined with high demand volatility. These require weekly review cycles, not automated reorder triggers.
Where commercial judgment overrode the algorithm
811 CZ SKUs flagged for rationalisation on algorithmic grounds. Applied a loyalty override: any SKU appearing on more than 3 invoices OR purchased by more than 2 distinct customers was protected. True rationalisation candidates after override: 19 SKUs (£886 total revenue). 756 protected.

A CZ-classified SKU may represent the sole reason a high-value account maintains its relationship with the business. The cost of losing a £50K annual account over a £12 SKU is asymmetric. The statistical classification cannot observe relationship value — commercial judgment must.
Limitations: no stock-on-hand data available — operates on demand patterns only · normality assumption violated by Z-class items (Poisson or bootstrap methods would be more appropriate) · single year of transaction data; seasonality effects may not be fully captured.

22% food cost gapSite 3: 37.0% · Benchmark: 28–32% · Root cause: purchasing behaviour
Data assembly
Square POS: inconsistent CSV exports per site with misaligned column headers. Deputy rota: scheduled and actual hours conflated. Waste log: physical notebook, free text, inconsistent categories, different handwriting per site — the messiest structured input I have worked with.

Built a standardised Google Form to replace the notebook going forward and coordinated back-entry of 8 weeks of historical data with site managers to establish a baseline. This data infrastructure work preceded any analysis.
Root cause elimination
Site 3 food cost: 37.0%. Sites 1 and 2 averaged 30.3%. UK industry benchmark: 28–32%. Persisted across all 8 weeks — structural, not episodic.

Waste log: Site 3 at 14.4% of revenue versus 2.7% at Site 1. Categories "over-prepared vegetables" and "prep excess" at 3–5× the rate of other sites.

Labour cost per cover: consistent across all three sites. Table turn: consistent. Revenue per cover: consistent. These alternatives were tested and eliminated. The gap is isolated to purchasing and preparation behaviour at Site 3.

Implication: if Site 3 matched Site 1's waste rate, food cost falls to approximately 31%. The required intervention is operational — manager coaching on prep volume calibration — not financial.
Limitations: data is synthetic, calibrated to published UK F&B operating benchmarks — in production, POS and rota API integration would automate ingestion · 8-week window may not capture seasonal variation in ingredient costs or covers.

Root cause
Instagram drives impulse purchases with insufficient product information at the point of decision. Return reason data: "Wrong size ordered" at 38% of Instagram returns (vs 32% email) and "Changed mind" at 22% (vs 10% email).

This is a channel-content mismatch — not a product quality issue. Fully-loaded return cost: £28.23 per unit (logistics, processing fee, markdown, handling). Instagram excess return cost versus email baseline: £23,630 annually.
Three interventions, sequenced by implementation complexity
1. Immediate (2 weeks): Integrate size guide directly into Instagram creative assets — addresses the information gap at point of purchase
2. Medium-term (1 month): Shift Instagram spend toward SKUs with low sizing-risk profiles (accessories, one-size items) — structural reduction without channel cut
3. Longer-term (3 months): Channel-specific £3 return contribution for Instagram-acquired orders — 65% of UK merchants now charge return fees
"Should they just stop spending on Instagram?"
No. Instagram drives brand discovery and top-of-funnel volume — acquired customers who don't return become repeat email purchasers. The objective is to fix the information asymmetry at purchase, not to reduce channel investment. Instagram remains a net-positive acquisition channel when the return cost is managed.
Limitations: data synthetic, calibrated to published UK D2C fashion return rate benchmarks — in production, Shopify order data joined to GA4 via UTM attribution · return reason categorisation assumes accurate customer self-reporting · £3 return fee requires price sensitivity testing before implementation.

Cluster-specific training reduced RMSPE by 29.7%. The improvement is consistent across all three model architectures — confirming that the gain comes from the methodology, not from any one model.
Data quality interventions (before any modelling)

Self-Directed Project

Smart Personal Finance Diagnostic

Central question: why does someone earning $883K over 5 years end up in financial deficit 67% of months? MECE issue tree across income, expense, behaviour, and structural branches. Before any analysis: caught all 146 Salary transactions labelled as "Expense" — understating income by $149,054. The kind of error that produces no error message and corrupts every downstream output.

Isolation ForestK-Means ClusteringHolt SmoothingPower BIExcel · 10 sheets
MECE issue tree — four branches, all tested
Income problem? No. $14,719/month. Growing faster than expenses in 3 of 4 years. Moderate concentration (HHI 0.374).
Expense problem? Yes. All 7 categories breach budget. Discretionary at 52.6% vs 30% benchmark. Correlation with net cashflow: r = −0.225.
Behaviour? Summer peaks, Friday highest. But trajectory improving: declining year-on-year since 2020.
Structural? Health score 26/100. Savings trajectory: improving +63pp over 5 years. Breakeven: projected January 2025.
ML methodology — honest reasoning for each choice
Isolation Forest over Z-score: Global Z-score found zero anomalies — because $1,900 Travel and $1,900 Personal Care are not equally unusual. Category-level normalisation made the 8 features meaningful. IF is multivariate; Z-score is not.

K-Means (K=2, Silhouette-optimal): Borderline months (42/60) vs Deficit months (18/60). Recovery months distinguished by lower wants spending, not higher income — discovered from cluster centroids, not constructed.

Holt Exponential Smoothing over ARIMA: 60 monthly data points is too few for stable ARIMA parameter estimation. Holt's is more stable with fewer parameters and easier to explain to a non-technical audience. 85.1% accuracy (MAPE 14.9%).

No supervised classification: 60 monthly rows would produce a severely overfit model. I am keeping that for datasets where N supports it.
