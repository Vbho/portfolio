Project 01 · Self-Built

Smart Personal Finance Tracker

Took a Kaggle personal finance dataset and treated it exactly as I would a real client engagement — structured the problem first, defined 12 KPIs before opening the data, then validated each one. The question wasn't "explore the data." It was: why does someone earning $883K over 5 years end up in financial deficit 67% of the time?

The answer: 52.6% of income was going to discretionary categories — nearly double the 50/30/20 recommended 30%. Income wasn't the problem; the trajectory was improving. Three targeted changes projected to flip −$3,250/month to +$1,200–$1,800/month within 6 months.

Before any analysis: caught a silent data quality bug — all 146 Salary transactions labelled as Expense, understating income by $149,054. The kind of error that doesn't crash your code but corrupts every number downstream.

How I structured the problem — MECE issue tree
Is the person in deficit because of an income problem? ├─ Is income too low? → No. $14,719/month is enough for 20% savings. ├─ Is income growing slowly? → No. Income grew faster than expenses in 3 of 4 years. └─ Is income too concentrated? → Moderate. HHI = 0.374. Salary is only 17%. Is it an expense problem? ├─ Which categories breach budget? → All 7. Every single one. ├─ Fixed vs variable split? → 29% fixed — healthy. Variable is the issue. ├─ Anomalous transactions? → 54 flagged by Isolation Forest ($33,338). └─ Is discretionary spend driving it? → Yes. Correlation r = −0.225 with net cashflow. Is it a behaviour problem? ├─ Temporal patterns? → Summer peaks, Friday is highest spend day. ├─ Is spending accelerating? → No. Declining year-on-year since 2020. └─ 50/30/20 compliance? → 17% needs, 18% wants compliance. Is it structural? ├─ Overall health score? → 26/100. Critical. ├─ Savings trajectory? → Improving — +63 percentage points over 5 years. └─ Forward outlook? → Breakeven projected January 2025.
Key feature engineering decisions
Added 20 columns to the original 5. The most important was Budget_Category — classifying every transaction as Needs, Wants, or Savings. That one column made the entire 50/30/20 framework possible. Without it, you just have transactions. The second most impactful was Is_Discretionary (binary flag for shopping, travel, entertainment), which isolated discretionary spend as a deficit driver in regression — correlation of −0.225 with net cashflow, consistent and directionally clear.
"Why category-level Z-score rather than global for anomaly detection?"
A global Z-score treats a $1,900 Travel transaction and a $1,900 Personal Care transaction identically. That's wrong — one is completely normal, the other is highly unusual. Category-level normalisation makes the Isolation Forest's features meaningful at the right scale. Running Z-score globally first produced zero anomalies. That felt wrong. Isolation Forest with 8 features including category-level deviation found 54 — the right tool for a multivariate problem.
ML methodology — honest reasoning
Holt's Exponential Smoothing over ARIMA: 60 monthly data points is on the low end for ARIMA. Estimating p, d, q parameters on a small sample produces high-variance estimates. Holt's is more stable, requires fewer parameters, and the output is easier to explain to a non-technical reader. 85.1% accuracy (MAPE 14.9%) — honest, not dressed up.
K-Means (K=2 by Silhouette Score): Two clusters — Borderline months (42/60) and Deficit months (18/60). The finding I wasn't looking for: recovery months are distinguished by lower wants spending, not higher income. That came from comparing cluster centroids. Genuinely discovered, not constructed.
Why no Random Forest or neural networks: 60 monthly rows is not enough data for supervised classification. Any model would overfit badly. I'm keeping those for a follow-up project with more rows — adding techniques for the sake of it would have been misleading.
What I'd do differently
Use real bank statement data — the auto-generated transaction descriptions meant I couldn't do merchant-level analysis (which subscriptions to cancel, which specific retailers are driving the overspend). That's the most valuable layer I was missing. I'd also add category-level forecasting, not just total-level — knowing Travel specifically is projected to spike in summer is more actionable than knowing total expenses will stabilise. And I'd personalise the budget benchmarks before running any utilisation analysis: $1,200/month for rent isn't realistic in London or Zurich.

Project 02 · MSc Coursework · University of Manchester

Rossmann Drug Store Sales Forecasting

"Forecast sales" is a task. The actual question: which factors drive daily sales variation across 1,115 German stores, and can that variation be predicted accurately enough to support real operational decisions on staffing, stock, and promotions? That distinction shaped every choice — including what the output files look like.

Key architectural decision: cluster stores by sales profile before training any model. One model trained across all stores averages fundamentally different populations and fits neither well. Training within clusters reduced average RMSPE by 29.7% — consistent across all three model types tested, ruling out a one-run artefact.

22.3% RMSPE on high-volume cluster (GBM), R²=0.727
−29.7% error reduction from clustering vs. global model
41,088 daily predictions + one-row-per-store summary for direct ops use

Data quality — caught before analysis
Two issues that would have silently corrupted results. Empty columns: two unnamed columns in stores.csv (Unnamed: 10 and 11) carried no data across all 1,115 rows — they came with the Kaggle download and would have been joined through without raising an error. Dropped immediately. StateHoliday type inconsistency: the column stored a mix of string "0" and integer 0. In Python these are not equal — filtering on df[df['StateHoliday'] == 0] returned roughly half the expected rows with no error raised. Any holiday-based feature built without fixing this would have been systematically wrong across 986,159 rows. Standardised to integers (0/1/2/3) before anything else ran.
Why RMSPE not RMSE
Why use RMSPE as the evaluation metric rather than the more common RMSE?
A forecasting error of €2,000 at a store doing €4,000/day is operationally catastrophic. The same error at a store doing €40,000/day is negligible. RMSE treats both identically — it penalises absolute errors. RMSPE penalises percentage errors, which is how store managers actually plan: staffing and stock decisions are proportional to expected sales volume, not absolute figures. RMSPE is also the official Kaggle metric for this competition — Rossmann considered it most relevant to their operational context.
Feature engineering — the three that mattered most
CompetitionOpen (months elapsed since competitor opened): raw CompetitionDistance is static. A competitor who opened 3 months ago is pulling customers through launch promotions; one established for 4 years is part of the local landscape. Converting opening date to months elapsed captures the disruption curve. CompetitionOpen consistently outranks raw CompetitionDistance in GBM feature importance.
LogCompetitionDistance: raw distance ranges 20m–75,860m — nearly 4,000x. GBM splits on thresholds; most signal sits at one extreme of an unlogged distribution. Log-transforming to 3–11 distributes signal evenly and lets the model detect the isolation premium: stores with distant competition tend to achieve higher daily sales, likely from reduced price pressure. Not reliably detectable in the raw variable.
IsPromo2Month: the raw Promo2 column only says whether a store participates in the mailing scheme. This feature flags whether the current month falls within a store's active mailing interval — distinguishing participation from active promotion at the transaction level.
Why GBM outperformed the Neural Network
The Neural Network produced the highest RMSPE (34.2% mid-volume, 32.7% high-volume) — below both GBM and Random Forest. This is consistent with how neural networks behave on structured tabular data with a modest training set: tree-based ensembles typically win here. The network trained for 50–200 epochs on a 40% sample, which wasn't enough. For context: a time-slicing approach (730-day sequential windows per store) generates ~182,000 training rows from the same data and has reached 8–9% RMSPE — but learns store-specific patterns rather than generalising, which trades interpretability for accuracy.
Output design rationale
Two output files, deliberately different. rossmann_predictions.csv: one row per store per day (41,088 records) — for technical validation. rossmann_store_predictions_summary.csv: one row per store with six-week total and daily average — for direct operational use. A store manager can open the summary file, read off their expected six-week revenue and daily average, and set staffing rotas and stock orders without needing to aggregate anything. The distinction between a model output and a usable deliverable matters.
Honest areas for further development
Lag features (yesterday, last-week same day, last-year same week) are among the most predictive variables in retail forecasting and weren't included — building them correctly requires careful data leakage prevention within a time-based split. Highest-priority addition. Full-data training run: all results use a 40% sample for development speed; a DEV_MODE flag exists to run on the full dataset. Full-data GBM typically reaches 15–20% RMSPE on this data, placing it in the upper range of Kaggle submissions. Finer store segmentation: K=2 is Silhouette-optimal but coarse — testing K=3/4 to assess whether the 17 Type B stores warrant their own cluster is the obvious next step.
Analytical Philosophy

How I Approach Every Engagement

A set of working principles I've carried from engineering into analytics — the ones that actually change the quality of the output.

① Define the question first. "Explore the data" is a task. "Why is this person in deficit 67% of the time?" is a question. One gives you charts, the other gives you answers.
② Pre-commit to metrics. KPIs defined before touching the data, not after. Choosing them afterwards is selection bias dressed up as analysis.
③ Check the data before trusting it. The worst errors don't crash your code — they silently corrupt your conclusions.
④ Tell clients what the data says. Not what they hoped to hear. A founder who thinks churn is a pricing problem needs someone willing to show it isn't.
⑤ The right tool is the one that gets used. Looker Studio over Power BI if the client lives in Google Workspace. Excel over Python if the output needs to be maintained by a non-coder.

SaaS · B2C · 10 days
Churn Analysis — 2,200-Subscriber Productivity App
Founder believed churn was a pricing problem. Segmented 14 months of Mixpanel data by tenure cohort, feature usage, and acquisition channel. Early churners (0–30 day) had never used the core feature — an activation problem, not a pricing one. Recommended Day-3 onboarding A/B test instead of repricing.
−18% churn30-day churn reduction over 8 weeks
Duration 10 days
Tools Python (Pandas) · Mixpanel export · Excel
What I actually did
Got 14 months of subscription data and Mixpanel behavioural events. Segmented churners by three tenure cohorts (0–30 days, 31–90 days, 90+ days), cross-referenced with feature usage patterns and acquisition channel. The 0–30 day cohort had near-zero usage of the product's core feature. That was the signal. The problem wasn't that users found pricing too high — it was that they never got far enough to see the value.
The hardest question I'd get asked
"The founder thought it was a pricing issue. How did you handle that pushback?"
I didn't argue with the hypothesis — I tested it. I pulled churn rates by pricing tier and showed him that the highest-tier customers churned at the lowest rate. Price wasn't the differentiator; engagement was. I let the data make the argument. He was actually relieved — fixing onboarding is cheaper than repricing, and it doesn't risk alienating existing customers.
Honest validation note
The 18% reduction is directional attribution, not controlled causation. The founder ran the email test to one half of new signups over 8 weeks while keeping the other half as control. No major external changes in that period. Good evidence, but I was clear in the handoff that this wasn't a randomised clinical trial.

D2C · Skincare · 4 weeks
Pricing Strategy — £1.4M Revenue Brand, 12 SKUs
Brand hadn't changed prices in 3 years and suspected they were underpriced. Three-part analysis: Van Westendorp PSM survey (n=340), 8-competitor benchmarking, and margin waterfall at current vs. proposed prices. Found they were 14–22% below the acceptable price ceiling on their top 4 SKUs. Their "premium" product was priced below mid-market competitors.
+3.2pp marginGross margin improvement within 90 days, no volume decline
Duration 4 weeks
Tools Excel · Typeform (Van Westendorp) · Competitor benchmarking
What I actually did
The Van Westendorp survey ran on the brand's email list (n=340) and identified the acceptable price range for their hero product. Competitor benchmarking covered 8 comparable brands — same product types, positioning, and channel mix. The margin waterfall showed the impact of proposed increases at current volumes. The key finding wasn't just that they were underpriced — it was that their "premium" positioning was being undermined by a price point below mid-market competitors, creating a confusing signal to customers.
The hardest question I'd get asked
"Van Westendorp has known limitations — it's stated preference, not revealed preference. How did you account for that?"
Fair critique, and I acknowledged it explicitly in the memo. The survey gives you a range, not a point. I used it alongside actual sales velocity data at their current price point and competitor pricing to triangulate rather than rely on it alone. The survey gave the ceiling; the market data gave the floor; the margin model gave the optimal point within that range. Any one of those three alone would have been insufficient.
Something I'd do differently
I'd deliberately include brands that attempted premium pricing and repositioned downward — not just benchmark against the successful premium players. Avoiding survivorship bias in the competitor set matters. I did this for this project, but it's something I now build into the brief from day one rather than as an afterthought.
Wholesale · Electrical · 2 weeks
Inventory Optimisation — 1,400-SKU Distributor
Company had 1,400 SKUs and was tying up capital in slow-moving stock while simultaneously stocking out on fast movers. ABC-XYZ analysis across all SKUs, rebuilt reorder points for the top 80 revenue lines. Identified 340 C/Z SKUs (low revenue, unpredictable demand) as candidates for rationalisation — but flagged 40 of them separately.

Duration 2 weeks

What I actually did
ABC-XYZ classification — A/B/C by revenue contribution, X/Y/Z by demand variability. Identified 340 C/Z SKUs as primary candidates: low revenue contribution and unpredictable demand. Rebuilt reorder points for the top 80 revenue SKUs using average lead time and safety stock calculations, using 18 months of demand data and supplier-stated lead times.
The hardest question I'd get asked
"ABC-XYZ is a standard framework. Where was the original thinking?"
The framework is standard. The judgment calls aren't. 40 of the C/Z SKUs were only ordered by 2–3 long-standing loyal customers who were high-margin in other product categories. I flagged those separately and explicitly recommended against discontinuing them on numbers alone — losing a high-margin customer relationship over a low-volume SKU is a bad trade. That's the difference between running a framework and doing analysis.
Honest caveat on the model
The reorder point model assumed lead times were consistent — which the client confirmed was broadly true except for one Chinese supplier. I flagged those SKUs as needing manual review. The model isn't perfect; it's a structured starting point that's better than the current gut-feel system, and I was clear about that distinction in the handoff.

Market Entry Feasibility — Gym Equipment Rental Startup
Founder wanted to launch a gym equipment subscription/rental service in London targeting home-gym users. Asked to validate the market and model the opportunity. Built TAM/SAM/SOM from ONS household data and fitness participation statistics; conducted 8 structured customer interviews from Reddit fitness communities to test willingness to pay and key objections.
Conditional GoFounder pivoted from VC fundraise to lean bootstrapped launch

What I actually did
The market existed but wasn't large enough to support venture-scale ambitions. I used Peloton UK's reported subscriber counts as a proxy for the addressable market ceiling, combined with ONS household and fitness participation data. The 8 customer interviews were recruited from Reddit fitness communities and tested willingness to pay, objections, and use-case fit. The hardest question I'd get asked
"Your recommendation was essentially against the founder's plan. How did you deliver that?"
I structured it as "the opportunity as currently scoped" vs. "the opportunity that's actually available." I didn't say don't do this — I said the market is X, not Y, and here's the business model that fits X. He could still build a good business; just not a venture-scale one. Founders hire analysts to tell them the truth. If I'd validated what he wanted to hear, I would have been useless — and he'd have raised money he couldn't deploy against a market that wasn't there.
On TAM model reliability
A TAM model is a structured argument, not a fact. I was explicit about every assumption, its source, and its sensitivity. I ran a scenario where my key assumptions were 50% wrong — was there still a viable business? The answer to that question told us more than the base case number ever could.
